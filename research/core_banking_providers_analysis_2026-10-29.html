Core Banking Providers — Full Competitive Analysis

13 platforms across four tiers · architecture, segment, commercial model, functional breadth and vendor viability

Dimension 1 — Platform Architecture and deployment: cloud-native or modernised legacy, multi-tenant SaaS or self-hosted, configuration-driven or code-driven, module boundaries.
Dimension 2 — Business Target segment, commercial and licensing model, functional breadth, implementation profile, and vendor viability — ownership, funding and consolidation risk.
Market structure
Four tiers, not one market
Vendors compete inside tiers, almost never across them
The tiers
Next-gen · Incumbent · Fintech-tier · BaaS
Cloud-native next-gen chasing tier 1–3 banks. Incumbent enterprise defending installed bases. Fintech-tier platforms serving EMIs and PIs on modest budgets. BaaS operators selling technology bundled with their own licence.
Where you actually compete
Fintech tier, with next-gen expectations
Your buyers are EMIs and PIs with fintech-tier budgets, but they shortlist against Tuum and Mambu and expect next-gen architecture. The tier you sell into and the tier you are measured against are different — that gap defines the product problem.
💡
Read this document with that asymmetry in mind. Thought Machine and Temenos are not your competition for deals; they set the architectural expectations your prospects arrive with. Advapay is your competition for deals, and it sets the price expectation. Tuum sits uncomfortably in between and is the single most dangerous name on this list for you.

Tier 1 — Cloud-native next-generation cores

Built this decade, cloud-first, sold to banks. They define what "modern" means in every RFP you will see.
Thought Machine Next-gen
Vault Core + Vault Payments · SaaS / self-hosted
Tier 1–3 banks and scaled fintechs · JPMorgan Chase, Lloyds, Standard Chartered, Intesa Sanpaolo
Architecture & deployment
Smart contracts on a universal engine
A deliberately product-agnostic Universal Product Engine: the core does not "know" the product it runs. Every product — current account, savings, time deposit, credit card, mortgage — is a Python smart contract on one event-sourced ledger. Vault Payments is a separate product, sellable to banks running other cores.
Segment & commercial model
Enterprise · developer-heavy
Enterprise licensing with substantial implementation partners. A Product Library of 200+ pre-written smart contracts shortens the start, but the operating model assumes in-house engineering capability.
Functional scope
Vault Core: ledger, product engine, posting, event sourcing — all account changes as immutable events
Vault Payments: standalone, ISO 20022 native, rails-based configuration, card issuing on Mastercard, dynamic account routing that decouples instrument from funding source
Named a Gartner Magic Quadrant Leader for Retail Core Banking in 2025, with both products recognised
Deployable as SaaS or self-hosted — important for banks with data-residency constraints
Strengths & cautions
Strength: the most architecturally coherent platform in the market, and the reference for payments-as-a-separate-product
Caution: every product is code. That is powerful for a bank with a platform team and prohibitive for a 20-person EMI
Caution: cost and implementation profile exclude the fintech tier entirely
Vault Payments selling into non-Vault-Core banks proves payments is a standalone market
💡
Implication for you: not a deal competitor, but the architectural benchmark your prospects cite. Your counter-position is explicit: configuration where they require code. An EMI should be able to launch a product without hiring a Python engineer — say that plainly, because it is the real difference and it is in your favour for this segment.
Mambu Next-gen
Multi-tenant SaaS · composable banking
Fintechs, digital banks, EMIs, credit unions · 260+ clients across 65 countries
Architecture & deployment
Composable, API-configured engines
Multi-tenant SaaS with lending, deposits and payments as independently licensable engines configured through APIs. Deposits is a single module spanning current accounts, savings, wallets and term deposits, with product type as a configuration parameter.
Segment & commercial model
Modular licensing, mid-market up
The clearest composable commercial model in the market: customers license the modules they need and expand. Mambu Payments launched in May 2025 following the acquisition of Numeral, with existing customers expanding onto it.
Functional scope
Deposits engine covers current, savings, group savings, wallets and term deposits, plus Islamic profit-sharing structures, in one configurable module
Lending engine with strong heritage in microfinance and consumer lending
Payments natively integrated with the core: routing updates GL accounts and handles AML suspense accounts automatically; SEPA CT/DD, UK Faster Payments, real-time payments
Broad connector ecosystem — customers historically integrated third-party PSPs and card providers through it
Strengths & cautions
Strength: configuration-driven, fast to launch, the broadest reference base in the digital-bank segment
Strength: the composable licensing model is the one to emulate commercially
Caution: the merged deposits module means a change to term-deposit maturity logic touches the same engine as current accounts
Caution: multi-tenant SaaS limits deep customisation — a recurring complaint from customers with unusual products
💡
Implication for you: Mambu is the commercial model to copy and the functional boundary to improve on. Its merged deposits engine is a simplification you have deliberately rejected; its composable licensing is a structure you should adopt wholesale. Expect Mambu in most competitive deals above the smallest EMIs.
Tuum Next-gen
Modular microservices · API-first SaaS · formerly Modularbank
EMIs, PSPs, fintechs and digital banks · LHV, Zenus, Solidgate
Architecture & deployment
True modules, sold separately
Modular microservices sold module by module or as a full core replacement. Core Banking covers accounts, transactions and FX; Cards, Lending and Payments are distinct modules. Deposits sit inside Core Banking alongside current accounts.
Segment & commercial model
Your segment, your pitch
Explicitly positioned for EMI and PSP flows, with modular licensing and progressive adoption. This is the closest architectural and commercial comparator to what you are building.
Functional scope
Payments across SEPA, SWIFT, FX and local rails with smart routing; pre-integrated with LHV Connect, Banking Circle, Currencycloud and CENTROlink
Upgraded during H1 2025 for ISO 20022, nostro reconciliation and inter-group payments with multi-currency nostro/vostro logic
Deposit products — fixed, notice and flexible — with automated lifecycle and interest logic inside the Core Banking module
Islamic finance deposit structures added in H1 2025; LHV's BaaS operation runs on Tuum
Strengths & cautions
Strength: the pre-integrated rail and sponsor connectors are a genuine moat — matching that list is a multi-year effort
Strength: a marquee reference (LHV) at exactly the institution type you target
Caution for them: accounts and deposits stay merged, forfeiting the EMI licensing upsell lever
Caution for them: no invoicing, receivables automation, loyalty or merchant-embedded credit — the business-layer gap is unaddressed
💡
Implication for you: this is the competitor to plan against, not Mambu or Thought Machine. Same segment, same architecture, same commercial model, already installed at your reference prospects. You do not beat Tuum on core banking parity. You beat it on the business layer — invoicing, collections, embedded credit — and on the DDA/Deposits split that gives EMIs a cleaner, cheaper entry price.
10x Banking Next-gen
SuperCore · "meta core" · SaaS on AWS
Tier 1–2 banks, building societies, BaaS operators · Chase UK, Westpac, Old Mutual
Architecture & deployment
Ledger + product engine, nothing else
A deliberately narrow scope: SuperCore is the ledger and ProductKit the product engine, with everything else reached through SmartAdapters and partners. Kafka-based event streaming enables real-time flow to partners without tight coupling. Polyglot customisation in any language.
Segment & commercial model
Enterprise, partner-dependent
Large-bank enterprise licensing. No native payments engine: payments go to specialist partners, with Paymentology pre-integrated for card issuing and processing. ISO 20022 support arrives through partner integrations rather than the core.
Functional scope
ProductKit abstracts deposit and lending products across retail, SME and corporate as one domain
Pre-built modules for savings, deposits and current accounts, all configured on one engine
Chase UK and Westpac run term deposits and current accounts on the same platform instance
Appears in Gartner's European retail core banking evaluations
Strengths & cautions
Strength: intellectual clarity — be the best ledger, partner for everything else
Caution: single pre-integrated card partner is a concentration dependency
Caution: no native payments engine is a real gap for payment-led institutions, which is most of your market
Tier 1–2 focus puts it outside your competitive set in practice
💡
Implication for you: a useful negative example. 10x's partner-only payments strategy works for banks that already have payment infrastructure. EMIs do not — payments is their business. Owning a native payments module is right for your segment precisely where it is wrong for 10x's.
Engine by Starling Next-gen
Bank-built core licensed as managed SaaS
Banks and licensed institutions launching digital propositions
Architecture & deployment
Proven in production at a real bank
Starling Bank's own core and digital banking stack, licensed to other institutions as managed SaaS. The whole proposition is that it runs a profitable licensed bank in production, not just a vendor demo.
Segment & commercial model
Full stack, bank-shaped
Core plus channels delivered together as a managed service. Appears in Gartner's European retail core banking evaluations. The product is shaped by Starling's own bank model, which is both its credibility and its constraint.
Functional scope
Core ledger, products, payments and customer-facing channels as an integrated stack
Built around UK retail and SME banking patterns, with deposit-taking assumed
Managed delivery model: the vendor operates the platform rather than handing over software
A growing category — bank-built cores sold outward also includes propositions from other digital banks
Strengths & cautions
Strength: production provenance at a real licensed bank is a credibility argument no pure vendor can match
Caution: shaped by a deposit-taking bank's needs — the EMI model is not its design centre
Caution: customers are buying from a competitor in the deposit market, a recurring commercial objection
Full-stack delivery means less freedom over channels and customer experience
💡
Implication for you: rarely in your deals, but it sets a bar on credibility. Your equivalent of "proven in production" is reference implementations and demonstrable operational transparency — which, under DORA, your customers need from you anyway.
Pismo by Visa Next-gen
Cloud-native core banking + issuer processing · owned by Visa
Large banks and scaled fintechs wanting core and card processing on one platform
Architecture & deployment
Core + card processing in one cloud stack
Founded 2016 in São Paulo and acquired by Visa for US$1bn (closed January 2024). Cloud-native, API-first platform on AWS covering accounts, ledger, credit, debit and commercial transactions, with connections to local rails such as Pix. Management and engineering still run as a separate unit inside Visa.
Segment & commercial model
Sold by Visa's global sales force to big issuers
Visa's stated goal is for Pismo to be the "platform of choice" for its issuing partners. Serves Mastercard-aligned issuers too. References are tier-one: Itaú (renewed 2024), Citi (corporate DDA, 2024), and historically Revolut, N26, Nubank and Cora. Europe went from no customers before the acquisition to one major bank live and three in implementation (early 2025).
Functional scope
Core banking ledger and accounts, including corporate demand deposit accounts (the Citi use case)
Issuer processing built in — credit, debit, prepaid and commercial cards on the same platform as the accounts, which no other Tier 1 core in this analysis offers natively
Rail-agnostic payment connections, including real-time local schemes
Live in Brazil, Mexico, Chile, the US, Europe, India and Australia
Strengths & cautions
Strength: Visa's balance sheet removes vendor-viability risk entirely, and its sales force puts Pismo in front of every large issuer
Strength: one vendor for core and card processing collapses the integration you would otherwise build to a separate processor (see Card Processing Providers)
Caution: scheme ownership — Mastercard-led issuers and regulators may question neutrality, and pricing will favour Visa-issued programmes
Caution: design centre is large banks; EMI-sized deals and European references are still thin
💡
Implication for you: the one Tier 1 vendor that bundles card issuing with the core, backed by the scheme itself. It will rarely meet you in small EMI deals, but it defines the "core plus cards from one vendor" expectation. Your answer is scheme neutrality — pluggable processors and BIN sponsors behind the Cards integration module — plus the business layer Pismo does not offer.

Composable cloud-native core · Türkiye
Retail, participation and investment banks, and fintechs — Türkiye, MENA and CIS
Architecture & deployment
Composable, API-first, cloud or on-premise
Founded in 2019, cloud-native and microservice-based with an API-first design — its own materials cite more than 5,500 API services and go-live in weeks. Two products: Fimple Core and Fimple Process Designer for configuration and workflow. Available as cloud SaaS or on-premise.

Regional challenger, modular licensing
$12m Series A announced February 2025, led by DN Capital and Smartfin with APY Ventures and Tera Portfolio participating. Core banking, digital banking and payments, with banking and lending modules licensed separately rather than as one front-to-back block

Tier 2 — Incumbent enterprise cores

Large installed bases, modernising under pressure. They shape vocabulary and expectations more than they threaten your deals.
Temenos Incumbent
Transact · microservices in progress · SaaS + on-premise
3,000+ banks in 150 countries · 41 of the top 50 global banks
Architecture & deployment
Legacy core, microservices overlay
Transact is progressively decomposing into independently deployable services. Payments came first, ahead of core banking microservices; the later "Accounts and Deposits" service manages current, checking and deposit accounts in real time as one unit, preserving the T24 boundary.
Segment & commercial model
Enterprise, modular adoption
Enterprise licensing with the largest installed base in the market. Microservices can be deployed in front of legacy infrastructure, so banks adopt incrementally rather than replacing wholesale.
Functional scope
The broadest functional footprint of any vendor here: retail, corporate, wealth, Islamic, treasury, payments, financial crime mitigation, analytics
Payments microservice integrates with SWIFT, SEPA and real-time schemes across 150+ countries and can overlay existing T24 estates
Accounts and Deposits supports any currency with a wide range of interest calculation methods and configurable business rules
Published benchmark of 150,080 TPS on Temenos Banking Cloud for a combined solution including payments, FCM, data hub and digital channels
Strengths & cautions
Strength: unmatched functional depth, geographic coverage and regulatory localisation
Caution: decades of architectural debt; the microservices evolution preserves legacy boundaries rather than rethinking them
Caution: implementation cost and duration place it beyond the fintech tier entirely
Its "Accounts and Deposits" naming is industry precedent for the merged model you are rejecting
💡
Implication for you: not a competitor, but a vocabulary-setter. When a prospect says "accounts and deposits" as one phrase, they are speaking Temenos. Your product naming has to work against that habit — be explicit that you split demand accounts from term deposits, and explain the licensing reason rather than the architectural one.

Suite licensing, everything bundled
Payments are a core component rather than a separately priced add-on. The universal banking philosophy bundles the full suite — the commercial model cloud-native vendors were created to displace.

Comprehensive single customer view across all product types in one system
Open APIs allow integration with third-party payment hubs despite the bundled model
Now integrated with Oracle Cloud Infrastructure AI services, though the payments architecture remains traditional

Strength: proven at enormous scale with deep regulatory coverage in emerging markets
Caution: architecture and commercial model both date from the universal-banking era
Notably, some EMIs run FLEXCUBE today — FinXP is a documented example
Least relevant comparator for your market, but a live source of displacement opportunities
💡
Implication for you: the clearest illustration of the model you are displacing — and a real pipeline. An MFSA-licensed EMI running an Oracle universal-banking core is paying enterprise cost for a platform designed for branch banking. Those institutions are findable and reachable.

What the landscape actually shows

The functional floor is settled. Every tier ships a ledger, multi-currency accounts, product configuration, payments and APIs. Nobody wins a deal on those any more, and nobody survives without them.
Payments separation is universal; accounts/deposits separation is not. Temenos, Thought Machine, Finacle, Mambu and Tuum all treat payments as a distinct product. Only Vodeno shows any catalogue-level split between current accounts and deposits — and even that may be presentational.
The tier boundaries are commercial, not technical. What separates Tuum from Advapay is not architecture quality — it is price, implementation model and who answers the phone. That is the boundary you have to pick a side of, deliberately.

Positioning conclusions

Price at the fintech tier, build at the next-gen tier. Your buyers have Advapay budgets and Tuum expectations. Modular licensing with a genuinely cheap entry module (wallet or DDA only) is how both get satisfied.
Tuum is the name to plan against. Same segment, same architecture, better references, pre-integrated with the sponsors in the companion payments analysis. Differentiate on the business layer and the licensing-aware module split, never on core parity.
Three reliable sources of pipeline: institutions outgrowing the BYOL tier, brands whose BaaS economics have stopped working, and EMIs running enterprise cores built for branch banking. All three are identifiable and reachable without a broad marketing spend.
Consolidation is the macro risk and the macro opportunity. Marqeta buying TransactPay, Finastra divesting its core unit, Treezor changing hands — every ownership change puts an installed base into play. Build the migration tooling early; it is a sales asset, not just an engineering convenience.
