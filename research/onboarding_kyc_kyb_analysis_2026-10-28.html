Onboarding KYC/KYB — Competitive Analysis

15 providers and routes for the Onboarding module · identity, business verification, screening and orchestration

Why this module is time-critical

Three regulations redraw European onboarding inside three years — and one of them has already moved sanctions screening off the transaction and onto the customer base.
Already in force
The Instant Payments Regulation (Art. 5d, SEPA Regulation as amended) bars transaction-level EU sanctions screening inside an instant transfer. The whole customer base is screened daily and on every list update instead, with an annual report.
End of Dec 2026
Each member state must provide at least one EU Digital Identity Wallet to its citizens. National rollout is uneven.
10 July 2027
The AMLR (Regulation (EU) 2024/1624) applies directly in every member state and repeals Directive 2015/849. One harmonised CDD regime replaces 27 national ones.
End of Dec 2027
Relying parties — banks and financial institutions included — must accept the wallet when a customer presents it, and register in the national register of relying parties.
2028
AMLA, based in Frankfurt, begins direct supervision of a first group of high-risk financial groups operating in at least six member states.
Q1 — Coverage & capability What does the provider actually cover — document and biometric identity, AML screening, business verification and UBO discovery, ongoing monitoring — and how much of it is its own data versus resold?
Q2 — Integration & commercial fit Does it fit a provider-agnostic orchestration layer, or does it want to own the journey? What is the pricing shape, and what is the minimum commitment an early-stage EMI has to swallow?
Five capabilities, not one
How to read this analysis
"KYC provider" conflates five distinct markets with different vendors, data moats and economics
The capability stack
IDV · Screening · KYB · Monitoring · Orchestration
1. IDV — document capture, NFC chip read, liveness, face match. 2. AML screening — sanctions, PEP, adverse media. 3. KYB — registry data, company structure, UBO discovery. 4. Ongoing monitoring — re-screening, perpetual KYC, event triggers. 5. Orchestration — the journey, routing, fallback, risk scoring, case management, audit.
Where your platform sits
Layer 5, never layers 1–4
Layers 1 to 4 are data and model businesses with real moats — registry licences, document libraries, screening lists, fraud telemetry across billions of events. Layer 5 is workflow, risk policy and audit trail, which is exactly what a core banking platform is good at and what the point solutions do worst.
💡
The central decision for your Onboarding module: build the orchestration layer and treat every provider as a swappable adapter. Multi-provider is not an architectural luxury — prospects arrive with existing contracts, pass rates differ by corridor and document type, and fallback routing between two IDV vendors measurably raises completion. A platform welded to one vendor loses deals on procurement grounds alone, and inherits that vendor's outages. Ask every vendor the licensing question before the technical one: may you embed the service and bill your own customer, or must the customer contract directly? Most of this market sells to the obliged entity, and the answer cuts the shortlist faster than any feature matrix.

Specialists — KYB and screening

Deeper than the full-stack platforms in one layer. Where the hard problems actually live.
KYB & UBO specialists Specialist
Registry data · ownership chains · Kyckr, Vespia, Creditsafe, Dun & Bradstreet, GLEIF
Any proposition with business customers — which means your entire Business Account module
Q1 — Coverage & capability
Primary-source registry data
Live company-registry retrieval across jurisdictions, officer and shareholder data, ownership-chain traversal to identify beneficial owners, and company-level monitoring. The value is the registry coverage and the graph traversal, neither of which a generalist IDV vendor builds well.
Q2 — Integration & commercial fit
Priced per lookup, per jurisdiction
Costs vary sharply by country because registry access terms vary — some registries are free and open, others charge per document. Budget per-corridor rather than per-check, and expect the economics to differ wildly between, say, Estonia and Italy.
What the AMLR demands here
25% threshold for beneficial ownership, direct or indirect, with control tested in parallel — so ownership percentage alone is not sufficient logic
Registers must be consulted and discrepancies between the register and what the customer declared reported within 14 calendar days — that is a workflow obligation with a clock, not a data lookup
A lower 15% threshold would require a Commission delegated act, which the Commission has indicated it will not adopt before 1 October 2027 — so build the threshold as configuration, not a constant
Customer data refresh at most every year for higher-risk customers and every five years for others, with records kept five years after the relationship ends
Implications for your platform
The ownership graph belongs in your data model. Entities, holdings, percentages, control relationships, evidence per edge and a computed UBO set — with history, because the question "who was the UBO when we onboarded them" is an audit question
The 14-day discrepancy report is a case type with an SLA. Build it as a first-class workflow with its own queue, not a note on the customer record
Multi-source is the norm in KYB: no single provider covers every jurisdiction well, so routing by country is mandatory rather than optional
The LEI (via GLEIF) is free, open and increasingly useful as a cross-reference key — worth supporting even though no vendor sells it
💡
Fit: KYB is the half to build properly, and the half everyone neglects. IDV is a commodity with five good vendors; UBO discovery through a four-layer cross-border holding structure is not. Since your Business Account proposition carries the highest ARPU in the value-proposition document and depends entirely on KYB, this is where the Onboarding module should be deepest — the ownership graph, the evidence trail and the discrepancy workflow, with registry data bought per jurisdiction.
Screening specialists Specialist
Sanctions · PEP · adverse media · ComplyAdvantage, LSEG, Dow Jones, Moody's
Every institution — screening is the one check with no risk-based exemption
Q1 — Coverage & capability
List quality is the product
Consolidated sanctions lists, PEP databases with family and close-associate linkage, adverse media with relevance classification, and update latency measured in minutes rather than days. The differentiator is false-positive rate at a given recall level, not list count.
Q2 — Integration & commercial fit
Bundled or standalone
Every full-stack platform in this document includes AML screening, so a standalone specialist is a deliberate upgrade — usually chosen when false-positive volume has become the compliance team's dominant cost, or when a supervisor has challenged the bundled list quality.
What the AMLR demands here
Customers, beneficial owners and controlling persons must be checked regularly against EU targeted financial sanctions — not only at onboarding
Enhanced monitoring for PEPs, with measures continuing at least 12 months after the person leaves office — a date-driven obligation the platform must track
CDD on new relationships and on occasional transactions at €10,000, with lower thresholds of €1,000 for transfers of funds and for crypto-asset service providers and €2,000 for gambling
AMLA's draft guidelines on ongoing monitoring closed consultation on 3 September 2026 and are not yet final — design for the direction, not the current letter
And one obligation that is not AMLR at all. Under the Instant Payments Regulation, a PSP offering SCT Inst screens its whole customer base at least daily and immediately on every list update, and is barred from screening EU lists inside the transfer itself. Non-EU regimes — OFAC, OFSI, national and trade measures — still require transaction-level screening where they apply
Implications for your platform
Screening is continuous, not an onboarding step. The architecture is a persistent subject register re-screened on every list delta, with alerts flowing into the same case queue as onboarding reviews
Store the match decision and the list version that produced it. "We screened them in 2027 and they were clear" is only defensible if you can show the list as it stood
The €1,000 transfer-of-funds threshold means screening is wired into the payment path, not just the customer record — which connects this module to Money Send and Instant Payments
Screening is therefore two ports, not one. A customer-base sweep with list-delta reprocessing and no latency budget, and a sub-second payment filter for the non-EU regimes. Different vendors, different SLAs, different legal bases — collapsing them into one interface is what makes an instant-payments deployment fail
False-positive reduction is the clearest, most measurable application of AI in the whole platform — and the one with a human decision point already built in
💡
Fit: bundle by default, support a standalone upgrade path. Start with the full-stack provider's screening and make the adapter boundary clean enough that a customer can swap in a specialist list provider later without touching the journey. That upgrade usually happens at the point a supervisor asks a hard question — and being able to answer it in a configuration change rather than a project is a retention event.
Licensed screening data Build-your-own
OpenSanctions · bulk dataset, on-premise, reseller licence
A platform that resells compliance rather than renting it per customer
Q1 — Coverage & capability
Sanctions and PEP, not adverse media
A consolidated, deduplicated sanctions and PEP dataset built from 470+ public sources, refreshed four times daily, delivered in bulk or deployed on-premise, ISO/IEC 27001 certified and already embedded under commercial products including Chainalysis and Quantexa. Adverse media is not in it — that stays a commercial feed.
Q2 — Integration & commercial fit
Flat-rate licence you may redistribute
An explicit reseller/OEM licence to embed the data in products you sell — the one route in this document that does not price per screened customer. The underlying data is CC BY-NC, so commercial use needs the licence, but the licence is flat-rate rather than per-subject.
Why this changes the economics
A per-subject screening contract scales with your customers' customers — a permanent margin tax on every institution you onboard, and one that is visible in your price list
A flat-rate data licence plus your own matcher turns screening into a fixed cost amortised across every deployment, improving with each new customer rather than eroding
It is the natural fit for the IPR daily sweep, where the requirement is completeness and list-delta speed over a known customer base and the matching problem is bounded
On-premise deployment answers the data-residency question outright, with no sub-processor to document
What you are taking on
Matching is the hard part, and it becomes yours. Transliteration, Arabic name order, corporate aliases, fuzzy thresholds and the false-positive rate are exactly what the commercial feeds are paid for
A weak matcher is a finding in your customer's supervisory audit, not yours — which is a harder conversation than a vendor outage
Fixed cost lands before the first customer does, which collides with a development budget cap — name the tension rather than discovering it at renewal
Adverse media and enhanced PEP still come from a commercial feed, billed to the institution as a per-customer add-on
💡
Fit: suite first for speed, licensed data later for margin — in that order. Ship Phase 1 on a bundled provider's screening to get a working product out, with every call behind your own interface so nothing in the core knows the vendor's name. Replace the screening layer with a licensed dataset once a second customer justifies the fixed cost. The reverse order spends capital before there is anything to amortise it across.

Non-vendor routes

Identity sources that are not a market. Both are obligations, and both arrive before 2028.
EU Digital Identity Wallet Obligation
eIDAS 2.0 · member-state issued · high assurance
Every relying party in the EU, including your customers
Q1 — Coverage & capability
Person identification data, high assurance
The wallet supplies verified person identification data — name, date of birth, nationality — at the "high" assurance level, issued by the member state. Under the AMLR, wallet-based identification is treated as equivalent to a face-to-face meeting for remote onboarding, with the detail to be set in technical standards.
Q2 — Integration & commercial fit
Free data, mandatory acceptance
There is no per-check cost and no vendor to negotiate with — but from end-2027 acceptance is an obligation, not an integration option, and relying parties must register in the national register of relying parties under Regulation (EU) 2025/848.
What it does and does not solve
Solves: the identity-proofing step for wallet-holding EU residents, at high assurance, with no document capture, no liveness check and no per-verification fee
Does not solve: screening, risk rating, affordability, post-onboarding fraud detection or KYB — one credible estimate puts the wallet at roughly a fifth of the total onboarding need
Rollout is uneven across member states, so journeys must accept the wallet and the existing document path indefinitely — not switch over
The AMLR separately allows verification via electronic identification at "substantial" or "high" assurance with relevant qualified trust services, so national eIDs matter too, not only the wallet
Implications for your platform
The journey engine needs a wallet path as a peer of the document path, selected per customer and per member state — this is a design assumption, not a feature to add later
Relying-party registration is an onboarding step for your customer as an institution. A platform that walks them through it is selling something no competitor currently offers
Adopt NFC chip reading now. A "high" assurance identity needs a trust anchor — either a face-to-face check or reading the chip in a recent passport or ID card — and private operators may read those chips with consent under Regulation (EU) 2025/1208. It is the same building block the wallet relies on and it serves everyone without a wallet during the transition
Dates here come from secondary analysis of the regulations; confirm against the primary texts before any customer commitment
💡
Fit: build the path before the obligation bites. This is the single clearest differentiation opportunity in the Onboarding module. Member states must offer wallets by end-2026 and relying parties must accept them by end-2027, which means every EU institution has a project ahead of it — and none of the core banking vendors in your competitive set markets wallet readiness today. Shipping a wallet-capable journey engine ahead of the deadline turns a compliance obligation into your sales opening.

Q1 — Coverage & capability verdict

IDV is a commodity; KYB is not. Seven providers do document and biometric identity well enough that the choice comes down to price, corridor, fraud resilience and data residency. UBO discovery through layered cross-border ownership remains genuinely hard — Onfido, the strongest IDV name here, does not offer it at all — and it is what your highest-ARPU module depends on.
The market is consolidating toward trust platforms. Veriff's acquisition of Vespia in February 2026 is the clearest signal: buyers want identity, KYC, KYB, screening and monitoring from one partner. Expect further consolidation and design adapters that survive it.
Screening is continuous, not a step. The AMLR requires regular re-screening of customers, beneficial owners and controllers, PEP measures lasting at least 12 months after office, and a €1,000 CDD threshold for transfers of funds and CASPs — which pushes screening into the payment path.
Screening has already moved off the transaction. The Instant Payments Regulation bars EU-list screening inside an instant transfer and requires the whole customer base to be screened daily and on every list update, while non-EU regimes still demand a transaction-level filter. That is two products with two latency budgets, and a vendor that only screens at onboarding satisfies neither.
The verification layer is itself under attack. With injection attacks up 40% year on year and deepfakes at roughly one in five biometric fraud attempts, provider selection is a fraud-resilience decision dressed as a pricing decision. Test adversarially.

Q2 — What this means for the module

Build layer 5, buy layers 1 to 4. Own the journey engine, the risk model, the ownership graph, the case management and the audit trail. Buy identity, screening and registry data as routed, swappable adapters. This is the same orchestration principle as cards and payment rails.
Two deadlines are the sales opening. Every EU institution must accept the EUDI Wallet by end-2027 and comply with the AMLR from 10 July 2027. No core banking vendor in the competitive set markets readiness for either. A wallet-capable, AMLR-shaped journey engine is a differentiator with a known expiry date — ship it before the obligation makes it ordinary.
Ask for embedding rights before you ask for a demo. Most of this market sells to the obliged entity, not to a platform that resells. Whether you may embed and bill your own customer decides the shortlist — and the three commercial shapes are not equal: pass-through earns nothing, resale earns a spread that erodes as the customer grows, and a flat-rate embedded licence is the only one that compounds across deployments.
Three AMLR obligations are workflows, not lookups: the 14-day registry-discrepancy report, the risk-tiered refresh cycle (annual for higher-risk, five-yearly otherwise), and meaningful human intervention in onboarding and refusal decisions. Each needs its own queue, SLA and audit record — and each is a feature the point solutions leave to the customer.
